# Every pair can agree while the whole differs

Every pair of reports agrees. Taken together, the reports still leave two incompatible worlds possible. How much of the whole did those pairwise agreements actually preserve? A tiny distribution gives the question an exact answer; a shared pool of resources reveals a different way locally satisfactory reports can fail to describe a working arrangement.

<a id="the-statistic-that-every-pair-loses"></a>

## The statistic that every pair loses

Two laws on three bits differ only in which parity they admit:

![The even law puts probability one quarter on 000, 011, 101 and 110, and the odd law on 001, 010, 100 and 111, yet every two-bit marginal is uniform under both.](https://causality.in/collections/composition-and-learning/from-unit-to-swarm/when-pairs-agree-and-the-whole-differs/joint-and-marginal.svg)

The upper panel shows disjoint joint supports; every omitted probability is zero. The lower panel shows what survives projection: each two-bit outcome has probability one quarter under both laws.

Look only at the first two positions. In either law, $00$, $01$, $10$ and $11$ each occur with probability $1/4$. For example, the first law's $00$ pair comes from $000$; the second law's comes from $001$. Dropping the third coordinate removes their difference. The same happens for either other pair. Every pairwise marginal agrees; the joint parity always differs.

Reconstructing the joint distribution from these marginals has no unique answer. In the relational version, joining the three pair projections admits all eight tuples, including the four the original relation excluded. The projections lost a constraint.

This is not the stronger claim that no global model exists. Both distributions are valid global models. Nor is it true that all labelled pairs of one concrete tuple lose that tuple: knowing its coordinate pairs determines its coordinates. Projecting a distribution or relation is a different operation from redundantly encoding one occurrence.

<a id="the-request-that-every-pair-permits"></a>

## The request that every pair permits

Three jobs each require four units from a pool of ten. Any pair requires eight and fits. All three require twelve and do not. A matrix of successful pair tests cannot authorise the aggregate allocation.

Here the missing object is not a higher-order probability law. It is a simultaneous reservation. Reading the same availability snapshot three times and approving each request separately does not create twelve units. The allocator must account for accepted reservations together.

The two examples have a shared lesson but different mechanisms. Statistical projection loses a joint constraint; separate capacity decisions fail to account for a shared resource. Treating both as generic “emergence” conceals what needs fixing.

<a id="a-binary-graph-can-still-represent-a-many-way-fact"></a>

## A binary graph can still represent a many-way fact

The issue is not a competition between graph and hypergraph names. A graph can introduce an occurrence node and link it to its participants through typed roles. Its identity keeps the participants attached to one event instead of allowing arbitrary recombination. The [W3C's n-ary relation patterns](https://www.w3.org/TR/swbp-n-aryRelations/) describe this established approach.

The encoding must preserve the constraint its user needs. Adding a node is not automatically sufficient: if its roles, time or occurrence identity disappear in an export, the export may again permit combinations the original excluded.

<a id="what-this-asks-of-a-swarm"></a>

## What this asks of a swarm

In [HENA](https://causality.in/hena/), a useful unit is not evidence of a useful composition. Units can make correlated errors, interpret messages differently, contend for memory or jointly exceed a budget. A task may depend on a joint relation none of their isolated summaries retained.

These two failures give a proposed HENA experiment concrete tasks: concurrent requests that contend for shared capacity, and an answer that depends on a joint fact its messages must retain. Comparing the swarm with simpler alternatives at matched resources would reveal whether its organisation helps preserve those relations. A [learned hierarchy](https://causality.in/collections/composition-and-learning/from-unit-to-swarm/emergence-needs-a-comparison/) would matter if its routing and memory carried those joint relations more effectively, rather than merely arranging units into nested groups.
