# The ineffable geometry

Okay, let's run a hypothetical thought experiment. You are participating in a tribe's discussion. There's a catch: you don't understand their vocabulary, and they don't understand yours. Where do you begin?

That is the difficulty of finding a mathematical language for causality: a relationship can seem within reach before there is a way to state it. It stops making sense pretty fast.

Mathematics and our understanding of the physical world have developed together. Calculus gives us a language for motion and change. Vector calculus helps describe fields. Tensor methods let us express geometry without tying everything to a particular coordinate system. The interesting part is not a tidy history in which each tool appeared for one purpose. It is that a better language can make a previously awkward question possible to ask precisely.

Could a richer geometric language help articulate causality, especially where quantum theory, complex systems and AI make ordinary intuition unreliable? What would that language let us see?

<a id="from-knowing-a-number-to-realising-what-it-means"></a>

## From knowing a number to realising what it means

Take another thought experiment. Suppose I tell you that a thousand children die each day for want of clean water. This is a stipulated number for the image, not a mortality estimate. You hear it and say: that's a lot.

Now imagine those thousand bodies lined up along your way home from work. Imagine that happening for ten years: $10\times365\times1000$ bodies, in this simplified count. Now imagine, God forbid, that one is someone you love.

The number has not changed. Your relationship to it has. Knowing, understanding and realising are doing different work here. The distance between a quantity and a lived consequence has become difficult to ignore.

The words *murti* and *darshan* suggest another way to approach that distance. In this image, the seeker comes to a form through a realisation, not merely through a label. “Idol” does not carry that distinction. This interpretation does not settle the words’ different religious meanings.

Geometry offered a mathematical counterpart to that wish: a way to see a relationship rather than only manipulate its symbols. A derivative becomes a slope; an integral becomes an accumulated area. The picture does not replace the definition or proof. It can help us realise what the definition is saying.

<a id="what-would-a-geometry-of-causality-let-us-do"></a>

## What would a geometry of causality let us do?

The ambition is a calculus whose geometry makes causal relationships more directly intelligible. Determinism is the holy grail here: an account precise enough to explain a mechanism rather than leave every question at a correlation. That aspiration does not establish that nature must be deterministic, or that probability and statistics have become unnecessary.

Indirect evidence seemed a useful starting point. We infer an unseen object from what it changes around it; the bending of light can reveal something about the mass between us and its source. Could a similar discipline help locate a causal relationship that cannot be inspected directly? Statistical causal inference already develops ways of asking such questions. The wider inquiry was whether insights from physics, mathematics, philosophy, computation and cognition could supply other useful descriptions.

Time symmetry was one possible direction. A law can admit a reversed description while the observations and boundary conditions available to us privilege one direction. Another was to make temporal relationships less dependent on the picture of an isolated present moving along a line. A third was simulation: specify a proposed rule, examine what follows, and find where the construction fails. A simulated effect would establish something about the rule supplied, not demonstrate that the physical world follows it.

Could a local mathematical account of intelligence let us isolate and transfer useful pieces of it? The idea of “micro-calculus” is to look closely enough at a system's changes to make a capability transferable in parts. The name supplies no method yet. Defining the unit, the preserved capability and the transformation would be part of the research.

That possibility matters beyond a clever model. If a system's reasoning and changes became more intelligible, it could help with reliability, engineering, public policy and decisions about AI. The chain from a new description to those applications remains work to establish.

<a id="more-than-a-line"></a>

## More than a line

Two days later, on 24 December 2023, I took the hunch into another thought experiment. This is my favourite kind: bring a few tangents together and see what breaks. Ready?

Is the sum of a triangle's interior angles always 180 degrees?

For an ordinary triangle in Euclidean plane geometry, yes. On a curved surface, the corresponding rule can differ. A triangle on a sphere, following great-circle arcs from the North Pole to two points on the equator ninety degrees apart, has three right angles. Its sum is 270 degrees. The surface and the meaning of its straightest paths are part of the question.

The comparison has a third case: on a hyperbolic plane, a geodesic triangle's angles add to less than 180 degrees. Positive, zero and negative curvature give different answers to the apparently familiar question. The [NASA comparison of a sphere, a flat plane and a saddle](https://imagine.gsfc.nasa.gov/observatories/satellite/wmap/shape.html) is a way into that distinction; specifying the geometry is what makes the answer precise.

Now ask: what is the dimension of time?

We know how to measure the dimensions of a phone or a room. Seconds, minutes and hours measure duration. But a unit of duration does not by itself tell us what kind of mathematical object time should be in a particular theory. Nor does uncertainty about that question mean that physics knows nothing about time.

For the sake of the obnoxious experiment, suppose time could be described using a fractional dimension and a non-Euclidean geometry. What would a line through that space look like?

In this image, it bends, loops, disappears and appears before it begins. Could understanding such a line tell us something about the flow of time, and could a framework make sense of an effect preceding its cause? Retrocausality was the question, not an established consequence of the picture.

Fractional dimension, curvature and causal order are different mathematical properties. Choosing the first two does not derive the third. To turn this image into a model, we would have to say what the space is, what counts as a path, how events are ordered, what observations are available and which equations relate them. Only then could we test consistency or ask what the construction predicts.

It is a challenging picture to wrap your head around. I don't blame you if it doesn't make sense. Hence the name *ineffable geometry*: the intuition comes before an adequate language for it.

<a id="a-hunch-with-work-in-front-of-it"></a>

## A hunch with work in front of it

As much as I hate to admit it, this is, at best, a hunch: the geometry of time might connect chaos, entropy, causality and consciousness. It needs mathematical rigour, beginning with a search for inconsistencies in its own assumptions.

The next exploration, [Entropy and a learning mind](https://causality.in/blog/entropy-and-a-learning-mind/), asks what happens when a trained system's useful initial arrangement keeps changing. A later, narrower calculation, [The future can constrain a model without sending a message back](https://causality.in/blog/future-without-retrocausation/), shows exactly how supplied endpoint data can constrain a whole path. There, the future enters through the data supplied to the problem. The larger question is how to describe time and causal order themselves.

[MONAD](https://causality.in/monad/) is where that idea of micro-calculus now lives, under the name it grew into: which answers a description preserves, and when the objects or rules of that description need to change. The geometric ambition is larger than any one existing example. Its value will depend on what it eventually lets us state, derive and test.

I am brewing the idea. The invitation is to explore it seriously enough to discover both what is promising and what does not survive the mathematics. The object of the thought experiment is more than a line. The line is where the work starts.
